Maple Grove Public Library — Homepage Redesign

Case Study
Subject & brief

A small-town library site, stuck in 2004

Maple Grove Public Library is a fictional stand-in for a genre of site that's real and extremely common: small municipal and library homepages built on old CMS templates, edited by whoever has time, and never redesigned. The pattern below (table layout, buried search, a wall of blue underlined links, no visual hierarchy) is representative of thousands of real small-town government and library sites still in production today.

01

Search is three clicks deep

The catalog search — the single most-used feature on any library site — isn't on the homepage at all. Visitors are instructed, in text, to navigate to another page first.

02

No hierarchy between urgent and archival

A closure notice, the library's mission statement, and a link to 2011 board minutes all carry equal visual weight. Nothing tells a visitor what's timely.

03

Fourteen-item top nav

Every department got a nav slot at some point and none were ever retired. Card catalog logic applied to a website: everything filed, nothing found.

04

Not usable on a phone

Fixed-width table layout with no responsive rules. On a phone this either scrolls sideways or collapses into an unreadable single column, depending on browser.

Step 2

Wireframe: reordering by what people actually come for

Before any visual design, the low-fi pass answers one question: what should exist on this page, and in what order? Catalog search moves to the very top, above the fold, unmissable. Everything archival (minutes, policies, forms) moves off the homepage entirely into their own pages.

Design rationale

  1. Search moves from "buried in a sub-page" to the first thing in the hero — it's the primary reason most visitors land on the site.
  2. A four-tile quick-action grid replaces the fourteen-item nav for the tasks that make up most real traffic: card sign-up, room booking, kids' programs, events.
  3. Events get a visual card treatment (date, title, one line of context) instead of a bare text link list, so the page communicates "this is alive and current."
  4. Hours move up and get a visible "open now / closed" state instead of requiring a visitor to read a table and do the math themselves.
  5. Everything archival — minutes, policies, forms, historical program pages — is deliberately cut from the homepage and pushed to dedicated sub-pages, reachable from the footer.
Step 3

Visual design: a reading room, not a filing cabinet

The direction leans into the library's physical identity — stamped due-date cards, catalog typography, reading-lamp green — rather than a generic municipal blue. Fraunces gives headlines warmth and a little friction; Space Mono stands in for the stamped, tactile parts of library life.

Step 4

What changed, and why it matters for use

A side-by-side of the specific usability failures on the old site and the decision made to fix each one — the connective tissue between "the before screenshot looked bad" and an actual, defensible design decision.

Old pattern Problem New pattern
01
Old

Catalog search lived on a separate "Catalog" page, reached via the top nav.

Problem

Search is the single highest-intent action on a library site. Hiding it costs every visitor an extra click and a moment of doubt.

New

Search sits in the hero, above the fold, with placeholder text that models real queries.

02
Old

Fourteen equal-weight top nav items, including departments most visitors never need.

Problem

A flat list with no priority forces visitors to read every label to find the one they want — high cognitive load for a five-second task.

New

Nav trimmed to five core destinations; the four most common tasks get dedicated visual tiles instead of nav links.

03
Old

A scrolling marquee notice and the library's mission statement given the same visual weight as a table of branch hours.

Problem

No hierarchy means visitors can't tell, at a glance, what's time-sensitive versus permanent — everything competes for attention equally.

New

A dedicated "open now" hours card with today's row visually distinct; events get date-forward cards, not inline text.

04
Old

A wall of blue underlined links for forms, minutes, and policies mixed in with the main content column.

Problem

Archival, low-frequency content pushes down the content people actually came for, and offers no visual cue about how current it is.

New

Archival material moves entirely off the homepage into footer-linked sub-pages, keeping the front page focused on this week.

05
Old

Fixed-width table layout, no responsive rules, footer note reading "Best viewed in 1024×768."

Problem

Most public-library visitors today are on a phone, often on a library-branch or limited data plan; a fixed-width table layout is effectively broken for them.

New

Fluid grid layout with defined breakpoints — quick-link tiles, event cards, and the hours panel all reflow to single columns on mobile.
